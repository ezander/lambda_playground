#* 
  Some notes on the language
  ==========================

  The choices for the design of the language were driven by the following goals
  and constraints:

    1. Stay as close to the original lambda calculus as possible
    2. Have very little predefined constructs
    3. Be expressive, fun to write, and easy to read
    4. Unambiguously parseable via some off-the-shelf LL-parser

  1. meant, that e.g. "λ x . x" should work. No "\" instead of "λ", or "->"
  instead of ".". One point of deviation: you can't write "λxy.yx", because
  identifiers are allowed to be longer than one character, so you need to write
  "λx y.y x" in that case, which seemed acceptable to me. That means you
  sometimes have to insert spaces, when copying from certain sources, but most of
  the literature can be copy-pasted almost verbatim.

  2. Only very few operator symbols have predefined meaning, like "λ", ".",
  ":=", etc., but not many more. You're free to define "0", "1", "2", "+",
  "∪", etc. however you want. That means you can choose yourself how to
  define your numerals, booleans, or lists.

  3. Expressiveness is on the one hand related to 2., i.e., having many operator
  symbols free to use, and on the other hand having easy input methods like e.g.
  "\gamma" for Greek symbols, or Alt+L for inserting λ. Also, the possibility
  to define infix operators via e.g. ":infix ∨" was driven by this, since
  "p ∨ q" is a lot better to read than "∨ p q".

  4. Also an important point to make the grammar easy and unambiguous. That's
  why e.g. there is no precedence (except for the normal abstraction to
  right/application to the left associativity). If you define infix operators
  via ":infix" like ":infix + *", then "1 + 2 * 3" will be evaluated as "(1 + 2)
  * 3", because infix just swaps operands/terms in an application (i.e. "a b" is
  changed to "b a", if b is infix and a is not) and can be done on the evaluation
  level, while explicit precedence would need extra parser logic.
*#

#*
  Associativity and Precedence
  ============================

  Parenthesis, associativity and precedence adhere to the following rules:
*#

# Outer parenthesis may be omitted
:assert λx . (M) ≡ λx . M

# Application is left-associative
:assert (M N) L ≡ M N L
:assert M (N L) ≢ M N L

# Abstraction is right associative
:assert λx . (λy . M) ≡ λx . λy . M

# Application takes precedence over abstraction
:assert λx . (M N) ≡ λx . M N
:assert (λx . M) N ≢ λx . M N

#*
  Substitution laws
  =================

  Substitution adheres to the following rules taken from:
    Gert Smolka: Untyped Lambda Calculus - Lecture Notes
    [https://www.ps.uni-saarland.de/courses/sem-ws15/ulc.pdf]

  Substitution rules including capture-avoiding substitution (last example):
*#

:assert (f x y)[x:=y] ≡ f y y
:assert (f x y x)[x:=z] ≡ f z y z
:assert ((λx.x) x)[x:=z] ≡ (λx.x)z
:assert (λx y.f x y)[f:=g] ≡ λx y.g x y
:assert (λx y.f x y)[f:=g z] ≡ λx y.g z x y
:assert (λx y.f x y)[f:=g x] ≡ λx' y.g x x' y

:assert x[y:=u] ≡ x
:assert y[y:=u] ≡ u

#*
  The following statements like this here...
*#
:assert (s t)[y:=u] ≡ s[y:=u] t[y:=u]

#*
  ... are a bit meaningless without explicit expressions for s and t, because
  this is meant to mean "for all lambda terms s and t it holds that...", but
  here s and t are just free variables, and we can't quantify over lambda terms
  here in the λ playground. Both sides in the assertion above always evaluate to
  "s t".

  So let's take some somewhat complicated expressions and plug them in for s and
  t. (Note: we make the defs local to this buffer by prefixing them with _)
*#

_s := x y u x y u λ x y u. x y u # term s containing x, y, and u
_s' := x u x u λ x y u. x y u    # term s not containing y
_t := a x y u c                  # term t containing x, y, and u and some free vars
_u := x y z                      # term u containing x, y and a free var z
_u' := y z                       # term u not containing x

#*
  Now the statements of course do not show, that the substitutions are correct
  for all lambda terms s and t, but they are meaningful now, and give you
  confidence that the substitutions are correct for all other terms as well. If
  you really need proofs, you need to turn to proof checkers like coq or agda,
  and not use a simple evaluation engine like this here.
*#
:assert (_s _t)[y:=_u] ≡ _s[y:=_u] _t[y:=_u]
:assert (λx._s)[y:=_u'] ≡ λx.(_s[y:=_u'])
:assert (λy._s)[y:=_u] ≡ λy._s
:assert λx._s' ≡ λy._s'[x:=y]
:assert _s'[y:=u] ≡ _s'
:assert _s[x:=x] ≡ _s
